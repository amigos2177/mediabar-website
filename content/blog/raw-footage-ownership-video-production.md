## The Short Answer: Who Owns Raw Video Footage?

Under standard U.S. copyright law, the video production company owns the raw camera files by default unless your contract explicitly transfers ownership or includes raw-footage delivery. Many production companies treat raw footage as a separate buyout—charging an additional fee after the shoot or refusing to release camera originals altogether.

## Why Raw Footage Ownership Matters for Businesses

Most companies do not think about raw footage until six to twelve months after a shoot—right when they need it most:

- **Updating a single detail without reshooting:** A logo changes, a product name updates, an executive moves into a new role, or a compliance disclosure needs a one-line revision.
- **Building a multi-format content library:** One well-planned corporate shoot can fuel web videos, LinkedIn clips, recruiting assets, trade-show loops, and investor decks long after the primary edit wraps. See our [multi-format corporate video strategy](/blog/corporate-video-multi-format-strategy).
- **Working with an internal marketing team:** Even when you love your production partner, your in-house designers or social managers may need clean B-roll clips for quick announcements.
- **Protecting long-term brand continuity:** If you film a landmark facility tour, an annual conference, or a retiring founder, that footage is a permanent company asset—not something you should have to rent back later.

## Common Raw Footage Policies Compared

Before signing a video production proposal, compare how different studios handle camera originals, project files, and third-party rights:

| Policy Model | What You Receive | Typical Risk for the Client |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Client-Owned Footage (Included)** | Finished masters plus organized camera originals captured for your shoot | Lowest friction; confirm hard-drive/transfer logistics and music licensing scope |
| **Raw Footage Buyout Add-On** | Finished masters only; raw camera files require an extra fee (often 20%–50%+ of production cost) | Unexpected budget surprise when your marketing team later asks for B-roll |
| **Agency-Retained Archive** | Finished masters only; raw footage stays exclusively with the agency | Vendor lock-in—every minor edit or clip pull requires going back through the original vendor |
| **Usage-Limited Commercial License** | Finished spots licensed only for specific markets, platforms, or time windows | Common for union/SAG talent or licensed music; must be separated clearly from raw footage ownership |

## Raw Footage vs. Project Files vs. Third-Party Licenses

Clear contracts separate four distinct items so there are no surprises during delivery:

1. **Raw Camera Footage (Camera Originals):** The unedited video and audio files recorded on set for your project. Confirm that these are included and delivered in an organized folder structure.
2. **Finished Deliverables (Masters & Cutdowns):** Your edited, color-graded, sound-mixed final exports in every agreed aspect ratio (16:9, 9:16, 1:1) and caption format.
3. **Editing Project Files:** Timeline files (Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, After Effects) often rely on proprietary plugins, paid fonts, and licensed templates. Ask upfront if you need editable motion templates or clean textless masters.
4. **Third-Party Rights (Music, Stock & Talent):** Owning your raw footage does not automatically mean you own unlimited global rights to a licensed music track, stock clip, or paid actor's likeness. Commercial talent usage and music licensing should always be itemized separately in your [custom production estimate](/pricing).

## Five Questions to Ask Any Production Company Before Signing

Use this checklist when comparing proposals from video production companies in San Antonio or across Texas:

1. **Do we own the raw footage captured on our shoot, or is there a buyout fee?**
2. **How are raw files delivered?** (Client-supplied drive, studio-provided drive, or cloud transfer—and how long does the studio keep backup copies?)
3. **Are textless masters or clean B-roll selects available if our team needs to make future updates?**
4. **Are any third-party music tracks, fonts, or on-camera talent agreements restricted by channel or time period?**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do some video production companies refuse to hand over raw footage?
Some agencies withhold raw footage to protect their creative reputation (worrying ungraded log footage looks unfinished) or to guarantee recurring editing revenue. While ungraded footage does require color and audio finishing to look broadcast-ready, a business that funded the shoot should still control its own assets.

### Will raw camera files play on a normal office computer?
Professional cinema cameras often record in high-bitrate codecs (such as ProRes, RAW, or 10-bit Log formats) that produce large files and look flat before color grading. If your team wants ready-to-browse clips alongside the camera originals, ask your producer to include lightweight H.264 proxy selects or a color-balanced B-roll reel in your delivery plan.
