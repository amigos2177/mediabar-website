Restaurant video production starts with what you want people to understand about your food and your business. That might be a signature dish, the preparation behind it, the people in the kitchen, or the experience of visiting your restaurant.

Plan the story and its intended use before choosing a filming schedule. A menu film, a recipe demonstration, and a commercial may need different preparation, footage, and finished versions. ## Keep the Food and Information Current

Use video to show dishes and experiences that match what guests can order or visit. Review older clips when menus, opening hours, locations, or seasonal offers change. Choose a posting rhythm your team can maintain and use audience response to decide what to make next.

A short update and a planned brand film serve different purposes. Everyday clips can show a special or a kitchen moment; a more developed film can introduce the restaurant, tell a recipe story, or support a launch. Choose the production approach around that purpose.

## What to Shoot - and How Often

You don't need a brand-new idea every day. You need a short list of reliable formats you can run on repeat:

- **The hero dish:** a tight, close shot of your signature plate - the pour, the slice, the steam, the cheese pull. Preview the finished dish in the first two seconds.
- **Behind the pass:** a quick look at the kitchen, the prep, the people. Show the preparation that helps explain your food.
- **The owner or chef on camera:** lead with personality. Let viewers hear who is behind the food.
- **The room:** show the dining environment alongside the food.
- **Specials and seasonal:** a new menu, a new cocktail, a holiday push - timely content that doubles as an announcement.

Choose a sustainable posting rhythm. Plan framing and length for the intended placement, use clear lighting, and make the featured dish easy to recognize.

When you want a sharper centerpiece - a launch spot, a brand film, a polished [commercial for a new concept or location](/video-production/commercials) - that's where a full crew earns its keep. Decide how everyday updates and planned campaign pieces fit your menu, team capacity, and budget.

## Plan the Filming and Finished Versions Together

Before filming, list the dishes, preparation steps, kitchen access, participants, and locations needed for the story. A restaurant shoot also needs to fit around service and the time required to prepare each dish for camera.

Decide where the finished video will appear and whether you need a main film, shorter edits, captions, or different aspect ratios. Those versions should be included in the brief and quote rather than assumed to come from one day of filming.

For a recipe film, plan the relationship between the ingredients, preparation footage, and finished meal. La Costeña’s Tostadas de Chicharron provides an example of that communication goal; your restaurant’s film should follow its own menu and message.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should a restaurant post video?**
Choose a schedule your team can maintain around service and menu changes. Review audience response and inquiries to decide which topics and posting frequency are useful.

**Do I need a professional crew, or can I film on a phone?**
Both can be useful. A phone can capture a timely update; a production crew can plan lighting, sound, preparation footage, and a more developed film. Choose the approach around the intended use and scope.

**What should a restaurant video show?**
Start with the dish or experience you want to explain. Preparation, kitchen activity, the finished plate, the dining room, and the people behind the food can each play a role. Plan the length and framing for the intended placement.
